It was never the format. It's the kernel.

2026-08-31 · Hongping Zhang · corrected 2026-09-03
Correction, 2026-09-03. This note first went out with numbers we labelled provisional, from 18 runs with no cooldown, a fixed execution order and no proof that the runs generated the tokens we asked for. We have since re-run the whole thing under a clean protocol — 45 runs, n = 5 per cell, three token lengths, randomized order, forced cooldown before every run, --ignore-eos, and the NVML hardware energy counter instead of an integrated power trace. The headline moved from −63.6% to −61.9% (−63.2% for the file MLPerf Client ships), so the suspected overestimate was real and small. One conclusion did change, and it sharpened the argument rather than weakening it: the quantized arms do not draw the same power as F16, they draw more. The text below carries the corrected numbers; what changed and why is set out at the end, and the superseded per-run CSV is still published.

MLPerf Client v2.0 shipped with signed Windows and macOS builds, agentic tasks, and a much wider hardware matrix — a lot of people can now benchmark a local LLM on their own machine without building anything. What comes out is speed: time to first token, tokens per second. Its power path is a different animal, needing a Yokogawa analyzer, SPEC PTDaemon and a second machine, so in practice a home run gives you throughput and nothing about joules.

We wrote a page about that gap last week, and it ended with a promise: our own energy numbers were measured with transformers + bitsandbytes NF4, while MLPerf Client's NVIDIA path is llama.cpp with a GGUF Q4_0 file, so the two could not be read against each other; putting a GGUF path next to ours was "the next thing on our list". This note is that measurement. It came back more interesting than we expected.

The result. On one RTX 4090, decoding Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, GGUF Q4_0 under llama.cpp costs 61.9% less energy per token than GGUF F16 under the same binary, for +5.6% perplexity. On the same card, bitsandbytes LLM.int8() costs 106% more energy per token than its FP16 baseline. Both are weight-only quantization of a 7–8B model on one GPU. The bit width does not tell you the sign of the energy effect. The kernel does.

What we ran

One rented RTX 4090 (Ada, 24 GB, 450 W limit), one llama.cpp build (b10643-192067b72, CUDA 12.x), one model, three GGUF files:

  • Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct-F16.gguf — the FP16 baseline that a benchmark suite of already-quantized models cannot give you;
  • ours-Q4_0.gguf — quantized by us from the same weights;
  • Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct-Q4_0.gguf — the file MLPerf Client v2.0 itself pulls for its NVIDIA llama.cpp path.

Each file was run at -n 64, -n 320 and -n 576, five times each, batch 1, -ngl 99 -fa 1 --no-mmap -c 2048 --temp 0 --seed 1234 --ignore-eos, in randomized order, with the GPU forced back to idle (~25 W, 27–30 °C) before every single run. Forty-five runs, all of which exited 0 and all of whose cooldowns converged. Energy is the NVML hardware energy counter across the whole llama-cli process; a 100 Hz power trace is recorded alongside it and integrated as a cross-check only. Quality is a separate llama.cpp perplexity pass over the WikiText-2 raw test set, 564 chunks at context 512, which touches the energy figure not at all — it was measured in the first session and not repeated here, on the same files and the same build.

The two token counts are the point. A raw process energy total includes loading the weights — 16 GB for F16 against 4.7 GB for Q4_0 — which hands the quantized arm a structural head start that has nothing to do with decoding. Subtracting the 64-token run from the 576-token run leaves the energy of exactly 512 additional decoded tokens, with load and prefill cancelling out. Every number below is that difference.

The third token length exists to check that this is allowed. If process energy is affine in the token count, differencing is exact; if it is not, differencing is a guess. Fitting E = a + b·n over all 15 runs of each arm gives R² ≥ 0.9966 and a slope that agrees with the differenced value to the digits we print, with the intercept (171–378 J) sitting where the weight load should be. The assumption survived its first real test.

The saving is throughput. The cheap arms actually draw more power.

Look at the decode power column before the energy column. F16 decodes at 273 W; the two Q4_0 arms decode at 296 W and 319 W — up to 17% more instantaneous power, on a card capped at 450 W. They are still 62–63% cheaper per token, because Q4_0 moves a quarter of the bytes per token and runs at 2.8–3.2× the tokens per second. Energy per token is power divided by throughput, and here the two terms pull in opposite directions with throughput winning by a mile.

This is the one place the clean rerun changed a conclusion rather than a digit. The first pass reported all three arms at 296–310 W and concluded "the power is the same, the speed differs"; with cooldowns and randomized order the F16 arm settles lower and the picture is sharper. A power-capped comparison would have shown none of this, and a throughput-only benchmark shows none of it either: you cannot get here without measuring both.

The same mechanism runs the other way in the case we reported for INT8: there too package power barely moved (74–91 W), and throughput fell to 0.63–0.72× of FP16, so energy per token went up. Which is the whole argument of this note:

Three implementations of "keep the activations, shrink the weights", one card, and the answer to "does quantizing save energy?" is +106%, −39% and −62% depending only on which kernel executes the result. LLM.int8() pays for its outlier-preserving mixed-precision decomposition in decode throughput; llama.cpp's Q4_0 path is a memory-bandwidth win on a bandwidth-bound workload and collects nearly the full ratio. A number like "4-bit" or "8-bit" predicts the memory footprint. It does not predict the sign of the energy effect, and anyone quoting an energy saving without naming the runtime is quoting a property of their kernel.

The quality axis does not rescue the ranking either. Ordered by energy the winner is llama.cpp Q4_0; ordered by perplexity damage it is LLM.int8() at +1.19%, which is also the worst on energy. There is no single "efficiency" ordering here, which is why this site publishes the two columns side by side and refuses to fuse them into one score.

A zero result worth publishing: our Q4_0 and MLPerf Client's are the same file, in effect

The two Q4_0 arms differ by 0.0002 in final perplexity — 7.7364 against 7.7366. Chunk by chunk across all 564, the largest absolute difference is 0.0003, and 472 of them agree to three decimals. On energy they differ by 3.5% in the clean run (1.8% if a single high 64-token outlier in the shipped-file arm is dropped), against a within-cell SD of 1.3–1.8%; in the first pass they differed by 1.4–2.3% and the sign of the gap flipped depending on which runs were excluded. A gap that small, that unstable across sessions, is a null result, not a ranking.

So: we are not claiming our quantization is more efficient than the one MLPerf Client ships. It isn't, within this measurement. What the comparison buys is provenance — the numbers in the table are not an artifact of one particular GGUF file, and anyone who quantizes Llama-3.1-8B to Q4_0 themselves should land where the shipped file lands.

What this does not show

  • This is not an MLPerf result and carries no MLCommons review or endorsement. It is an independent research measurement that happens to use the same model file. MLPerf Client's own power methodology measures wall AC power for the whole system with external instrumentation; we measure GPU package power via NVML. Those two boundaries are not interchangeable and the numbers must never be subtracted from each other.
  • n = 5, one card, one session. The site's own repeatability note puts run-to-run CV at a few percent but the gap between two of our sessions at 100+ points; a 62% effect is far larger than either, but the third digit is not defensible.
  • The token counts are inferred, not read back. llama.cpp's timing line still did not parse in our wrapper, so a run is credited with the tokens it was asked for whenever --ignore-eos was set and the process exited 0. With --ignore-eos that is a sound assumption — and it is what removed the impossible-bandwidth artifact of the first pass — but it remains an assumption rather than a measurement.
  • The two energy accountings disagree by about 15% per run. The NVML hardware counter reads 14.9% higher than trapezoidal integration of the same run's 100 Hz power trace (range 2.5–31.8%). The counter is what we report, because it integrates in hardware rather than between samples. After differencing, which cancels the part that is common to both runs, the two accountings agree to 0.2–4.3% — so the headline is robust to the choice, but a reader building an at-home energy tier out of sampled telemetry should know that a 100 Hz trace is not the same instrument as a counter. Both columns are in the per-run CSV.
  • Perplexity was not re-measured. The +5.6% is from the 2026-08-31 session, on the same three files and the same llama.cpp build. It is a property of the weights, not of the run protocol, but it has not been repeated under the clean protocol.
  • Decode phase, batch 1, single stream, one prompt length. No prefill-heavy or agentic workload, no concurrency, no CPU, DRAM, PSU, PUE or CO₂e.
  • The cross-backend comparison in the figure holds the card constant but not the model (Qwen2-7B against Llama-3.1-8B), the runtime, or the generated-token count. Read the sign and the mechanism from it, not the exact magnitudes.

If you are running MLPerf Client v2.0 this week

The two things measure different quantities and compose rather well on one machine. Run the signed client for speed on the workloads MLCommons has defined; run the container for the joules and the perplexity of a precision pair you choose, with the FP16 baseline measured in the same session:

docker run --rm --gpus all -v "$PWD/out:/out" \
  ghcr.io/hongping-zh/ecocompute-mlcube:latest \
  --model Qwen/Qwen2-7B --precision NF4 --output_dir /out

Keep the boundary in view when you put the two reports side by side: client tokens/second is a whole-system, vendor-optimized number; our joules are GPU-package only. And the container's current backend is transformers + bitsandbytes, so it reproduces the method of this note, not the llama.cpp rows — a GGUF backend, which is what would let anyone reproduce the table above with one command, is now the top of our list. All forty-five runs are in the per-run CSV linked above, with the undifferenced process energy under both accountings, the cooldown state, the first and peak power sample and the sample count on each, so you can redo the arithmetic differently; drop your own result on the replications page, especially if it disagrees.

One thing to watch when reading that deposit against this note: the Zenodo abstract quotes whole-process energy per output length (−55% to −61%, and the two Q4_0 arms within 1.2% of each other at 320 and 576 tokens), whereas everything here is decode-only, after differencing the weight load out (−61.9% / −63.2%, the two arms 3.5% apart, which is dominated by the 64-token cell).
